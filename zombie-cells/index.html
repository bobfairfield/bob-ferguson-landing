The Zombie Cell Problem

Why aging seems to speed up in our later years, and what your cells need to push back.

Almost everyone over 65 knows the feeling. A cold that once lasted three days now lingers for three weeks. A bruise takes a month to fade. Stairs that were nothing are now something. It feels like aging picked up speed.

It did, and scientists now understand why.

Its main point: aging does not run in a straight line. It speeds up when damaged "zombie cells" build up faster than an aging immune system can clear them. Credit to the channel for explaining it so clearly. Below, we tie that idea to the published research, and to what Vivix+™ does about it.

Cells that stop working but won't die

Every cell can divide only so many times. When a cell reaches that limit, or is damaged along the way, it usually does not die. It enters a state called senescence. Researchers nicknamed these cells zombie cells.

A zombie cell stops doing its job but stays in your tissue. Worse, it sends out a steady stream of inflammatory signals. Those signals damage the healthy tissue around it and can push neighboring cells to become zombie cells too.

This matters. In a landmark 2011 study, clearing zombie cells from aging mice delayed age-related decline in their muscles, fat, and eyes.1 Mayo Clinic has since shown that the number of zombie cells can be reduced in people.2

Why they pile up

Zombie cells build up when they form faster than the body clears them. Both sides of that balance shift as we age.

More of them form. DNA damage, shortening telomeres (the protective caps on our chromosomes), and inflammation all push cells into senescence. Nobel laureate Elizabeth Blackburn and psychologist Elissa Epel showed that chronic stress speeds up telomere shortening.3

Fewer get cleared. Our immune system normally removes zombie cells. As it ages, it falls behind, and zombie cells accumulate faster.4

And they drain your energy. In 2020, teams at the Buck Institute and Mayo Clinic discovered that zombie cells switch on an enzyme called CD38 in nearby immune cells. CD38 destroys NAD+, the molecule your cells need to make energy and repair DNA.5,6 Less NAD+ means less repair, which means more damage. The loop feeds itself.
